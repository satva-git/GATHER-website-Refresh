Transfer Pricing and Intercompany Accounting: A Practical Guide for Multi-Entity Finance Teams

How transfer-pricing policy connects to intercompany recharges, accounting entries, reconciliation and Group reporting for finance teams using Xero and QuickBooks Online.

  • How recharges work

Transfer pricing is the process of determining the prices charged for transactions between related companies within the same Group.

For multi-entity finance teams, however, defining an appropriate transfer-pricing policy is only part of the challenge.

The Group also needs to implement that policy consistently across its accounting records.

This can involve identifying intercompany costs, calculating allocations and mark-ups, creating corresponding entries across multiple legal entities, managing different currencies and reconciling the resulting intercompany balances.

For Groups using Xero or QuickBooks Online, those processes can quickly become dependent on spreadsheets and manual accounting entries.

What is transfer pricing?

Transfer pricing determines the pricing applied to transactions between related companies.

Common examples of intercompany transactions that may be subject to transfer-pricing policies include:

  • management and head-office charges;
  • shared employee costs;
  • IT and software costs;
  • marketing expenditure;
  • professional fees;
  • intercompany services;
  • intellectual property charges;
  • loans and interest; and
  • goods transferred between Group companies.

Depending on the nature of the transaction and the Group’s transfer-pricing policy, one entity might recharge another at cost, apply an agreed mark-up, or use another pricing methodology.

Transfer pricing is therefore closely connected to intercompany accounting.

The transfer-pricing policy determines how a transaction should be priced. The finance team then needs to ensure that the resulting transaction is correctly recorded across the relevant Group companies.

How do transfer-pricing recharges work?

Consider a parent company that incurs £100,000 of central costs on behalf of five subsidiaries.

The Group’s transfer-pricing policy requires eligible costs to be allocated between those subsidiaries using an agreed methodology and recharged at cost plus 5%.

The finance team may need to:

  1. identify the expenditure covered by the transfer-pricing policy;
  2. determine which subsidiaries should bear the costs;
  3. calculate the appropriate allocation;
  4. apply the 5% transfer-pricing mark-up;
  5. create the intercompany recharge;
  6. record the corresponding transaction in each counterparty;
  7. translate transactions where entities operate in different currencies; and
  8. reconcile the resulting intercompany balances.

The transfer-pricing calculation itself may be relatively simple.

Executing it accurately across multiple accounting entities is often the harder part.

Why are transfer-pricing recharges often managed in spreadsheets?

Many multi-entity Groups use spreadsheets to manage intercompany cost allocations and transfer-pricing calculations.

A spreadsheet might identify costs incurred by the parent company, allocate them between subsidiaries and calculate the required cost-plus mark-up.

The resulting entries then need to be posted into Xero, QuickBooks Online or another accounting system.

This approach can work well for smaller Groups.

But as the number of entities, currencies and intercompany relationships increases, the spreadsheet can become an increasingly important part of the Group’s financial-control environment.

Finance teams may need to establish:

?Which underlying transactions were included?

?Which allocation methodology was used?

?Were the corresponding entries recorded in every counterparty?

?Which entities were charged?

?What transfer-pricing mark-up was applied?

?Do the resulting intercompany balances reconcile?

This is where transfer pricing becomes more than a tax calculation.

It becomes an intercompany accounting and financial-control process.

What is a cost-plus transfer-pricing recharge?

A cost-plus recharge applies an agreed mark-up to a defined cost base.

For example, if one Group company provides £50,000 of qualifying services to another company and the applicable transfer-pricing policy requires a 5% mark-up, the resulting recharge would be:

Where several subsidiaries receive centrally provided services, the Group may first need to allocate the underlying cost base between the relevant entities before applying the appropriate mark-up.

The complexity can increase further where different types of expenditure have different allocation methodologies or transfer-pricing treatments.

What is the difference between transfer pricing and intercompany accounting?

Transfer pricing and intercompany accounting are closely related, but they are not the same thing.

Transfer pricing determines the appropriate pricing methodology for transactions between related entities.

Intercompany accounting records, reconciles and ultimately eliminates transactions and balances between companies within the Group.

A Group may therefore have a well-designed transfer-pricing policy but still have an inefficient intercompany accounting process.

For example, the tax or transfer-pricing adviser may establish that particular services should be recharged using a specified cost base and mark-up.

The finance team still needs to turn that policy into accounting entries across the relevant entities every month.

Can transfer-pricing recharges be automated?

Many of the recurring accounting processes associated with transfer-pricing recharges can be automated.

Rather than manually identifying expenditure and rebuilding allocation spreadsheets every month, finance teams can establish rules determining:

  • which transactions should be recharged;
  • which Group companies should receive the recharge;
  • how costs should be split or allocated;
  • which mark-up or discount should apply; and
  • how the corresponding intercompany accounting entries should be created.

Automation can be particularly valuable for Groups with recurring management charges, shared-service arrangements or centralised costs.

The objective is not to automate the professional judgement required to establish an appropriate transfer-pricing policy.

It is to automate the repeatable accounting process required to execute that policy.

Does GATHER.nexus determine the correct transfer-pricing policy?

No.

GATHER automates the accounting implementation of a transfer-pricing policy; it does not determine what that policy should be.

The appropriate transfer-pricing methodology depends on factors including the nature of the transactions, functions performed by the relevant entities and applicable tax rules.

Groups should obtain appropriate professional tax and transfer-pricing advice when establishing their policies.

Once that policy has been determined, however, finance teams still need a reliable way to execute it.

That is the problem GATHER is designed to help solve.

Bringing transfer pricing and intercompany accounting together

Transfer pricing is often discussed primarily in terms of tax compliance, documentation and methodology.

For a Group finance team, there is an equally practical question:

How do we turn our transfer-pricing policy into accurate, repeatable accounting entries across every relevant Group company?

For a small Group with relatively few transactions, spreadsheets and manual journals may be perfectly adequate.

As the Group grows, however, more entities, currencies, allocation rules and intercompany relationships can make the process increasingly difficult to manage manually.

Connecting transfer-pricing calculations with intercompany recharges, counterparty accounting, reconciliation and Group reporting can turn what was previously a collection of spreadsheet processes into a controlled multi-entity workflow.

And that can make transfer pricing considerably easier for the finance team to operate month after month.
