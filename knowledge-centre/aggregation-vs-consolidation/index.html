Aggregation versus consolidation in group reporting

Aggregation adds company figures together. Consolidation adjusts those combined figures to present a parent and its subsidiaries as one economic entity. The distinction matters because internal sales, loans, ownership and acquisitions can make an unadjusted total very different from the group accounts.

What aggregation means

Aggregation combines data from more than one company into a shared view.

It can help a finance team compare entities, see total cash balances or assess the scale of activity across a portfolio.

The data may already have been mapped into common reporting lines and converted into a single currency.

Those steps improve comparability, but they do not by themselves complete consolidation.

An aggregated revenue figure can still include a sale from one group company to another.

An aggregated balance sheet can still include both an internal receivable and the matching payable.

What consolidation adds

Consolidation reflects the group as a single economic entity. Under IFRS 10, control determines which subsidiaries are consolidated, subject to the standard’s scope and exceptions.

The process combines the relevant balances and applies the required adjustments.

These can include eliminating internal transactions and balances, offsetting the parent’s investment against subsidiary equity, and recognising acquisition and ownership effects.

The precise requirements depend on the applicable accounting framework and the facts.

A management report can use a different scope or presentation, but it should make that basis clear.

The differences at a glance

QuestionAggregationConsolidation
What is the starting purpose?Combine company figuresPresent the group as one economic entity
Are internal transactions removed?NoRequired intragroup eliminations are applied
Is investment against subsidiary equity addressed?Not simply by adding balancesRelevant investment and equity elimination is applied
Are acquisition and ownership effects addressed?Not inherentlyRequired adjustments are included
What does the total represent?Combined figures on the stated basisThe consolidated result on the stated basis

A worked example of revenue and expenses

Assume Parent Ltd owns 100% of Subsidiary Ltd and controlled it throughout the period.

Both companies use sterling and consistent accounting policies.

Parent Ltd has £900,000 of external revenue and £100,000 of management income charged to Subsidiary Ltd. Subsidiary Ltd has £600,000 of external revenue.

Parent Ltd has £700,000 of expenses. Subsidiary Ltd has £450,000 of expenses, including the £100,000 internal management charge.

There are no other consolidation adjustments in this simplified profit and loss example.

Profit and loss itemAggregatedAdjustmentConsolidated
Revenue£1,600,000(£100,000)£1,500,000
Expenses£1,150,000(£100,000)£1,050,000
Profit£450,000£0£450,000

The internal charge is income in one company and an expense in the other. Removing both reduces reported revenue and expenses by the same amount. It does not reduce profit in this example.

This is why a group can overstate its external revenue while still reporting the correct total profit. A plausible bottom line is not evidence that the consolidation is complete.

The balance sheet needs separate attention

Now assume the £100,000 management charge is unpaid at the reporting date. Parent Ltd records an intercompany receivable and Subsidiary Ltd records the matching payable.

An aggregated balance sheet includes both amounts. Consolidation removes the £100,000 receivable and £100,000 payable because the group cannot owe that money to itself.

The journal debits the intercompany payable and credits the intercompany receivable. This is separate from eliminating the management income and expense.

If the balances disagree, investigate the difference before treating the elimination as complete.

Other balance sheet adjustments, including the parent’s investment against subsidiary equity, would also be needed for a complete consolidation.

This example isolates the internal trading balance.

Why some consolidation adjustments change profit

Not every elimination leaves profit unchanged. Suppose one company sells inventory to another for £120,000 when its cost was £90,000.

If all that inventory remains unsold outside the group at period end, the £30,000 internal profit has not yet been earned from an external customer.

Assuming no impairment or other adjustments, consolidation reduces inventory and group profit by £30,000. Internal sales and purchases also need the appropriate elimination.

This illustration excludes tax and non-controlling interests. In practice, consider the applicable deferred tax requirements and ownership effects.

The important distinction is that eliminating a matched service charge and eliminating profit held in inventory solve different problems.

Partial ownership is not a percentage roll up

If a parent controls a subsidiary but owns less than 100%, full consolidation generally includes all the subsidiary’s relevant balances.

The interests attributable to other shareholders are presented separately as non-controlling interests.

For example, an 80% holding in a controlled subsidiary does not normally mean adding only 80% of its revenue.

Equally, ownership below 50% does not automatically rule out control. The assessment follows the applicable framework.

Associates and joint arrangements should not be assumed to follow the same approach as controlled subsidiaries.

When an aggregated view is useful

Aggregation can be useful for operational comparison, early close reviews and clearly defined portfolio reporting. It can also be a visible intermediate stage in a controlled consolidation.

The risk arises when readers assume an aggregated view is a consolidated result. Label the basis, explain exclusions and make clear whether internal activity and ownership effects have been addressed.

For board reporting, decide what question each report answers.

A view comparing entity performance may deliberately retain internal charges, while a consolidated group view removes the relevant internal activity.

How to check what a group report actually does

Ask the preparer or software provider to demonstrate one internal transaction from both entity ledgers through to the final report.

Then inspect the investment elimination and one ownership or acquisition adjustment relevant to your group.

  • Can you distinguish combined entity figures from consolidated figures?
  • Can you identify each material elimination and its supporting calculation?
  • Can you explain remaining intercompany balances and differences?
  • Does the report reflect acquisition dates and changes in control?

These checks are more useful than relying on the label “group reporting” alone.
