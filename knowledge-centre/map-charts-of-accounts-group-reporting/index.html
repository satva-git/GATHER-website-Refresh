How to map different charts of accounts for group reporting

Account mapping connects each company’s chart of accounts to a common group reporting structure. It allows entities to retain useful local detail while the group reports consistently. A good mapping process controls completeness, classification and change, so the finance team can explain where every material balance appears.

Start with the group report you need

Define the group reporting structure before mapping individual accounts. Agree the profit and loss and balance sheet headings, management subtotals and level of detail needed by the board, lenders and finance team.

A group reporting template is not necessarily a copy of the parent’s chart of accounts.

The parent may be a holding company with little trading detail, while operating subsidiaries need more revenue and cost categories.

Use consistent definitions for the reporting lines. If one entity treats a cost as direct delivery expense and another treats the same cost as overhead, a combined gross margin becomes difficult to interpret.

Keep local accounts and group classifications distinct

Local ledgers support day-to-day accounting, statutory requirements and operational analysis. Group mappings determine how those balances appear in the common reporting format. They need not use identical codes.

Where a source account contains several types of expenditure, one account-level mapping may be too coarse.

Consider whether the entity needs a more detailed chart, a supported transaction-level split or a documented adjustment. Avoid implying precision that the source data does not contain.

This distinction becomes important when acquired companies join the group.

Replacing every local account on day one may disrupt operations; mapping provides a way to establish group reporting while the wider integration is planned.

An example across three entities

Assume the group wants a single software and hosting line. The local accounts below can map to that line even though their codes and descriptions differ.

US company725 Cloud platform costsSoftware and hosting
Australian companyIT04 SaaS licencesSoftware and hosting

The reviewer should check the contents of each account. A label such as “IT costs” might also contain equipment, staff costs or consultancy that belong elsewhere.

One group line can receive multiple local accounts. The reverse requires care: splitting one source account across several group lines needs an allocation method supported by the available data and the reporting tool.

Map the balance sheet as carefully as the profit and loss

Profit and loss classifications attract attention because they affect management performance measures. Balance sheet mapping is equally important for working capital, debt and consolidation.

Identify intercompany accounts separately from external receivables and payables.

Distinguish investments in subsidiaries from other investments, and review equity accounts, tax balances and financing arrangements.

Check current and non-current presentation where relevant. A description such as “loan” does not establish maturity, counterparty or accounting treatment.

The mapping should follow the facts.

Separate mapping from accounting policy adjustments

Mapping changes presentation; it does not automatically correct differences in recognition or measurement.

If entities apply different capitalisation policies, moving their accounts to the same reporting line will not align their reported profit.

Identify material policy differences and record the appropriate group adjustments.

Keep these distinct from the mapping so reviewers can tell whether a movement reflects reclassification or a change in measurement.

The same principle applies to local GAAP differences and acquisition fair-value adjustments. Account mapping is one layer in the reporting process, not a substitute for consolidation judgement.

Control completeness at each close

Reconcile the source population to the mapping register. Every account with relevant activity or a balance should have a reviewed outcome, including any deliberate exclusion.

  • Review new accounts created since the last reporting period.
  • Investigate unmapped balances and accounts mapped to catch-all lines.
  • Check unexpected debit or credit balances and unusual classification changes.
  • Confirm that closing balances and period movements reconcile to the source ledger.

An unmapped account should be an explicit exception to resolve. A report can look plausible while omitting an entire class of costs.

Make mapping changes understandable

Record the source entity, account identifier, description, target line, rationale and effective date where relevant. Assign responsibility for reviewing material changes.

If a mapping changes, assess the impact on comparatives.

Current-period expenses moved from overheads to direct costs can create an apparent margin movement unless prior periods are presented consistently or the difference is explained.

Keep a record of the basis used for the reviewed report. Reapplying today’s mapping to an earlier period can otherwise change the historic presentation without an obvious explanation.

Use automation with a review process

Rules and AI-assisted suggestions can reduce repetitive work, especially where a new entity has many accounts.

They should be treated as proposed classifications that need proportionate review.

Prioritise high-value, ambiguous and unusual accounts. Similar descriptions can conceal different transactions, while different descriptions can represent the same economic item.
