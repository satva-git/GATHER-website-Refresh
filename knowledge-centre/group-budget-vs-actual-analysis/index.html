Group budget versus actual analysis explained

Group budget-versus-actual analysis compares reported performance with the agreed plan, then explains the differences. A useful review identifies which part of the group caused a variance, why it happened and what action follows. The calculation is a starting point; the explanation is what makes it useful to management.

Make sure the comparison is like for like

Use the same period, reporting lines and business scope.

Comparing a monthly actual with a quarterly budget, or a group that includes a new acquisition with a budget that excludes it, creates differences that need separate explanation.

Check whether figures are monthly, year to date or full year.

A cost delayed by one month can produce a large monthly variance while leaving the annual expectation unchanged.

Use the agreed budget as the reference point.

If management revises expectations, identify the new forecast separately so readers can distinguish the original commitment from the latest outlook.

Calculate the variance and state the convention

A straightforward arithmetic convention is actual minus budget.

Percentage variance is that difference divided by the budget, where the budget is non-zero and the result is meaningful.

A positive difference is not always favourable. Higher revenue may be favourable; higher costs are usually adverse.

Labels should make the business meaning explicit rather than relying only on a plus sign.

For zero budgets, a percentage variance is undefined. For negative budgets or unusual balances, the percentage can be confusing.

Use the absolute amount and a clear explanation.

A worked operating profit example

Assume the following monthly group figures. Expenses are displayed as positive costs. Favourable and adverse labels describe the effect on operating profit.

MeasureBudgetActualDifference
Revenue£500,000£530,000£30,000 favourable
Direct costs£300,000£330,000£30,000 adverse
Overheads£120,000£125,000£5,000 adverse
Operating profit£80,000£75,000£5,000 adverse

Revenue is 6% above budget, but operating profit is £5,000 below budget. The extra £30,000 of revenue has been offset by £30,000 of additional direct costs, with another £5,000 spent on overheads.

Gross margin has fallen from 40% to approximately 37.7%. Reporting only the revenue increase would miss that deterioration.

Investigate whether the additional work had a different mix, lower pricing or higher delivery cost.

Look below the group total

An acceptable group result can conceal significant differences between business units. Suppose Unit A is £40,000 above its profit budget and Unit B is £45,000 below.

The group variance is only £5,000 adverse, but the required management discussion is much larger.

Investigate material differences by the business classifications used in the budget, such as region, unit or product line.

Keep central costs visible so they do not disappear into an unexplained residual.

Drill-down should help identify where a difference sits. It does not establish the operational cause without reviewing the supporting facts.

Separate timing differences from changes in performance

A delayed marketing campaign can create a favourable cost variance this month without reducing the expected annual spend.

An unfilled role may create a saving but also delay delivery or revenue.

An activity difference can require a different interpretation from a cost-control problem. Higher variable costs may be expected if sales volume is above plan.

A fixed annual budget remains useful, but the explanation should recognise the change in activity.

Where needed, prepare a separate volume or price analysis using supporting data. Do not imply that every variance-reporting system automatically calculates these operational bridges.

Explain FX and changes in group scope

If a foreign operation is translated at rates different from those assumed in the budget, some variance may reflect currency rather than local trading.

Identify the rate basis and separate the effects where material.

Acquisitions, disposals and reorganisations can also change the comparison. Provide a clearly labelled like-for-like view where useful, with the adjustments explained.

The reported result should remain identifiable. An analytical adjustment should not silently change the financial reporting basis.

Turn commentary into a management decision

For each material variance, explain the amount, cause, expected duration and proposed response. Assign a responsible person and a next review date where action is required.

A useful comment might read: “Delivery costs are £18,000 above budget because two projects used external contractors. £10,000 relates to work brought forward from next month; £8,000 is an expected overspend. The delivery lead will review contractor cover before the next close.”

This is more informative than “costs above budget”. It distinguishes timing from an enduring change and identifies what management can do.
