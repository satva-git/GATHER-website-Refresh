Consolidation working papers explained

Consolidation working papers show how individual company accounts become the group accounts. They bring together source balances, mappings, currency translation and consolidation adjustments so that a reviewer can understand and test the reported result. For a growing group, they are the evidence behind the numbers.

What consolidation working papers should explain

A consolidated report presents the answer. The working papers explain how that answer was reached.

For each material figure, the finance team should be able to identify the contributing entities, the source accounts and the adjustments applied.

This matters when a CFO challenges a movement, an auditor requests support or a new team member takes over the close.

A total that cannot be reconstructed creates avoidable investigation and dependence on the person who built it.

There is no single mandatory spreadsheet layout for every group.

The useful test is whether the documentation supports the reporting framework, the complexity of the group and the review being performed.

Build a clear bridge from entity balances to the group

A useful starting structure shows the mapped entity balances, an aggregated total, identifiable adjustments and the consolidated balance.

Where currencies differ, the translation process must also be visible.

Keep the meaning of each column clear. If a report displays both aggregation and consolidation, label them explicitly so readers do not mistake an unadjusted total for the final group result.

Working paper componentQuestion it answers
Entity balancesWhich companies and accounts contribute?
Mapping and translationHow were those balances classified and converted?
Consolidation adjustmentsWhat changed at group level and why?
Consolidated balanceWhat amount appears in the group report?

A worked example of an internal management charge

Assume a parent and its wholly owned subsidiary have been in the group throughout the reporting period and both use sterling.

The parent has £800,000 of external revenue and charges the subsidiary £80,000 for management services. The subsidiary has £500,000 of external revenue.

Revenue lineParentSubsidiaryTotal
External revenue£800,000£500,000£1,300,000
Internal management income£80,000£0£80,000
Aggregated revenue£880,000£500,000£1,380,000

The revenue elimination is £80,000, giving consolidated revenue of £1,300,000. The matching expense in the subsidiary is also removed.

The consolidation journal debits management income and credits management expense by £80,000.

The working papers should link that entry to the internal charge and the counterparty record.

If the invoice is still unpaid, the corresponding receivable and payable require a separate balance sheet elimination. Eliminating income and expense does not remove the outstanding balance.

Retain the backing calculation for each material adjustment

A journal amount is not a full explanation. The support should identify the facts, method, inputs and accounting rationale that produced it.

For an acquisition, this could include the consideration, acquisition date, ownership, identifiable net assets and fair-value adjustments supporting goodwill.

For an unrealised profit adjustment, it could include the internal margin and the inventory still held within the group.

For foreign currency translation, retain the relevant rates and the movement explaining the translation reserve.

For non-controlling interests, retain the ownership basis and the allocation of net assets and results.

The calculation should be understandable without reverse-engineering an unexplained number or relying on a conversation with its preparer.

Connect the report to its underlying evidence

Traceability has several layers. A reviewer may need to move from a group reporting line to a company balance, then to a mapped account and the source transactions.

A group-only adjustment follows a different path: from the reported figure to the journal and its supporting calculation.

Both paths matter. Access to source transactions explains the entity data; it does not by itself explain goodwill, an ownership allocation or a consolidation-only adjustment.

Where software supports drill-down, check its actual depth and scope.

Where evidence sits outside the system, use clear references and a controlled location so the reviewer can still follow the chain.

Keep source corrections separate from group adjustments

A missing supplier invoice belongs in the relevant entity books. An elimination of a valid internal charge belongs in consolidation.

Confusing the two can cause entity accounts and group reports to drift apart.

Use descriptive journal narratives and categories. Identify the period affected and whether a recurring entry needs a new amount, a reversal or a reassessment.

Repeating the previous month’s journal without checking the underlying facts can preserve an error.

For every material adjustment, the reviewer should know whether it corrects source data, aligns accounting policies or arises solely because the businesses are being presented as a group.

Review completeness as well as arithmetic

A balanced working paper does not prove that every company, account or adjustment has been included.

Review the perimeter, reporting dates, new accounts and ownership changes before relying on the totals.

  • Reconcile imported balances to the entity records.
  • Check new and unmapped accounts and unexpected classification changes.
  • Investigate unmatched intercompany balances and unexplained FX movements.
  • Reconcile opening balances and explain significant movements in reserves.
  • Confirm that material journals have support and that the final report agrees to the reviewed working paper.

Preserve what was reviewed

If source data or assumptions change after review, the resulting report may differ from the version that was approved.

Keep a record of the reviewed period, data cut-off and material subsequent changes.

The method may involve controlled files, exports or system features. The requirement is practical: the team should be able to identify what was reviewed and explain any later revision.

Good documentation reduces the effort needed to answer questions later. It supports an audit, but it does not replace audit procedures or guarantee an audit outcome.
